title: Radical acceptance, a first look: this is my brain
description: What radical acceptance from DBT looks like for an ADHD, autistic, or AuDHD brain: stop fighting what is already true, grieve what needs grieving, and practice it in one breath.
chapter: From Pathways to Presence

Remember Alex reading that report more than once? That was the start of radical acceptance.

Dr. Marsha Linehan, who made DBT, has a short way to say it:

> **Pain + not accepting the pain = suffering** (Linehan, 2015).

Radical acceptance means you stop fighting what is already true. Not about everything. Not forever. Just this one true thing, right now. "Radical" means all the way down: in your head, and in your body too.

In this chapter, the true thing is your brain.

A lot of us spend years at war with it. Buying the fifth planner. Promising to "just try harder." Forcing ourselves through the loud party with no break. Hating ourselves for needing the fidget, the list, the quiet room. That fight is the suffering part. It doesn't change your brain. It just wears you out.

Radical acceptance sounds like:

- I'm AuDHD. I need a list for things other people just remember. So I'll make the list, and skip the speech about how I shouldn't need one.
- I'm autistic. Phone calls cost me a lot. So I'll text when I can, and plan a rest after the calls I can't skip.
- I found out at 22. I can't get those years back. I can start from here.

**What it's not.** Accepting your brain doesn't mean you like every part of it. It doesn't mean you stop trying meds, tools or support. It doesn't mean you accept being mistreated, or that the world gets to stay built for someone else. You can accept your brain and ask for accommodations. Honestly, acceptance usually comes first. It's hard to ask for help with something you're still pretending isn't there.

**Skill or mask?** Acceptance is for you. "I'm autistic, and loud rooms drain me" is acceptance. "I just have to hide it all day so nobody's uncomfortable" sounds like acceptance, but it's the mask talking. If you're not sure which one you're doing, use the four questions from "Why fitting in is so tiring": Who is this for? Did I choose it? Can I stop? How do I feel after?

**Grief is part of it.** If you found out late, accepting your brain can mean grieving the years you didn't know. The grades you blamed yourself for. The people who called you lazy. The friendships that fell apart and you never knew why. That grief makes sense. Acceptance doesn't skip the grief. It goes through it.

**It won't stick the first time.** You'll accept your brain on Monday and be yelling at it by Wednesday. That's normal. DBT calls coming back to acceptance, again and again, turning the mind. Chapter 6 teaches it, along with a Queer Pathways map for this: Philadelphia or Toronto? Say where you really are first.

**Try it, for one breath.** Say one true sentence about your brain. Out loud, in your head, or typed into your notes app. "My brain is ______." No "but" after it. Then let your hands open, or your shoulders drop, or press your feet into the floor. Whatever your body likes.

That's it. You just practiced radical acceptance.
