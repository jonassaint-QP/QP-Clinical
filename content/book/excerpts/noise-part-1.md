It began, as it so often did, with the hum. It wasn't a sound, exactly. It was a vibration that lived just under Alex's skin, a low, full-body static that had been there for as long as they could remember. On good days it idled like a car at a red light, easy to forget. On days like this one it climbed into their teeth and split their thoughts into a hundred loose threads.

The task in front of them was the Q3 campaign analytics report. It sat open on their laptop: forty tabs of click-through rates and cost-per-acquisition tables, the part of a creative director's job that nobody puts in the posting. Alex could build a brand from a single word scrawled on a napkin. They could walk into a pitch with nothing and walk out with the account. But a spreadsheet asked their brain to crawl through mud, and their brain, built for novelty and run on interest, simply would not go. They knew the report mattered. They could picture it finished. The problem was never knowing what to do. It was the gap between knowing and doing, the place the psychologist Russell Barkley calls the point of performance, and for Alex that bridge had been washed out for years.

Their supervisor, David Sterling, did not have this problem. David was brisk and linear, a man who saw a task as a point on a map and progress as a straight line toward it. Alex admired that. They also felt quietly judged by it, as if the way their mind moved, sideways and in orbits, were a character flaw rather than a difference in wiring.

The 10:00 a.m. deadline had not snuck up on them. It had been sitting in their calendar all week. They had looked right at it that morning, and then, somewhere around 7:15, they had clicked on a video about Japanese joinery. Three hours later they knew the difference between a kanawa tsugi and a shachi sen, and they had watched a man in Kyoto assemble a kumiko lattice with no glue, no nails, and no hurry.

Somewhere in the second hour the scarf joints had stopped being about wood. Alex had a legal pad covered in sketches of a suspension bed: a hardwood frame that held itself together by tension alone, strong enough to take the full weight of a body in surrender and never buckle. Sex didn't belong in a shameful drawer or bolted together in the dark. It deserved joinery this beautiful, built to be seen.

For those three hours the hum had gone silent. Every piece fit. Effort led to result. The world made the kind of sense that campaign analytics never did. That was the bargain of their brain: hours of absorbed, perfect peace, paid for with the ordinary work left undone. In the moment, it always felt worth it.

At 10:31 their phone buzzed against the desk, and the jolt went straight through their sternum. David's name. Their heart picked up. They opened the email already knowing what it would say, and it was still worse.

> Subject: Fwd: URGENT: Q3 Analytics Report & Headshots
>
> Alex,
>
> The deadline for the Q3 report was 10 a.m. this morning. It is now 10:30 a.m. Need it by noon, LMK if that will be a problem. Also, see below. Let Brandon know your status for Tuesday.
>
> David
>
> ---Begin Forwarded Message---  
> From: Brandon Walsh (Corporate Events)  
> To: David Sterling  
> Subject: Corporate Headshots - Your Team's Availability
>
> Hi David,
>
> Just a reminder that corporate headshots are next week. I've attached the schedule. I just need to confirm if Alex will be available for his allotted time slot on Tuesday so we can finalize with the photographer.
>
> Thanks,  
> Brandon

And just like that, the hum became a siren. There it is. Two hits, one email. The first was familiar: proof, again, that they couldn't manage the basic work of being an adult. The second was quieter and cut deeper. His allotted time slot. Maybe Brandon didn't know better. David did. David had read it, forwarded it, and not thought it worth a correction. One small word, and they were gone.

The shame arrived before any thought did, hot and acidic, climbing from their stomach into their throat. Their body had read the email as a predator long before their mind could weigh it. Their heart hammered. The edges of the office blurred to gray. A cold sweat broke along their hairline. Somewhere far back in the brain, beneath language, the oldest alarm had made its call: danger. Run, or fight.

And then the voices came. They were old voices, a chorus of every sighing parent and exasperated teacher and smirking classmate Alex had ever known, pressed together over the years into a single presence. Alex had a name for it. They called it the Auditor.

Failure, it said. You had one job. Lazy. Three hours of videos about wood. Listen to yourself. Fraud. They're going to figure it out. You talked your way into this job, and now they can see what you really are. And then, softest of all: They don't even see you. To them you're just a fuck-up in a man's suit. He. He. He.

Run, or fight. There was nowhere to run. So they fought. The paralysis broke all at once and their hands went to the keyboard. Not to write an explanation. They opened the report, and the spreadsheet that had been a wall for three days turned, under the whip of terror, into a road. This is the dark side of an ADHD brain: panic can do what interest won't.

Seventy minutes later, the report was done. They attached it. They typed the first line with steady fingers. Then the anger that had been simmering under the fear boiled over, and they kept typing.

"Q3 report attached. I apologize for the delay. P.S. My pronouns are they/them. A reminder that using the correct pronouns is not optional; it is a baseline requirement for a respectful and inclusive workplace. Using 'he' is inaccurate and harmful. I would appreciate it if you would make a better effort in the future."

The cursor hovered over Send. Somewhere far back, a small, sane voice said: Wait, kid. Not now. Breathe first. But the part of them at the wheel was fighting for its life, and to that part, sending felt like the only way to let the pressure out.

They clicked. For one bright second there was relief. A clean, electric high. They had fought back. They had named themself. The siren stopped.

It lasted about five seconds. Then their stomach dropped, the way it does when you miss a step in the dark. The heat drained from their face. They reread the email, this time through David's eyes: a one-line apology bolted to a lecture, sent by the person who had just missed the deadline. Not a boundary. A receipt. Proof, in writing, that Alex was unreliable and unstable both.

What have you done? The point was right. The timing was a grenade. They had known better, and they had pulled the pin anyway. I am my own worst enemy, they thought, and the thought rang through them like a struck bell.
