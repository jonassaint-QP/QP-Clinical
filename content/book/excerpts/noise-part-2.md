The sent email sat on the screen. Alex wasn't reading it anymore. The words had come apart into black marks.

What came next was older than the email and older than David. Alex knew it the way you know the first ache of a flu. The panic had nowhere left to go, and so their body did what bodies do with a threat they cannot escape: it began to shut down.

Polyvagal theory describes this as the dorsal state, the nervous system's oldest emergency brake. Not fight, not flight. Stillness. Collapse. Playing dead.

Cold moved up through their legs. Their arms grew heavy, as if their sleeves had filled with wet sand. The colors of the office faded. The sounds of the floor, the printer, someone laughing down the hall, went muffled, as if heard from underwater. Their body felt very far away, a heavy coat they were no longer wearing.

The Auditor did not shout now. It didn't need to. It spoke in the flat, patient voice of someone reading evidence into a record.

Alex had a private name for what happened next. The Internal Courtroom. It convened without being called, and it always ran the same way: the Auditor at the prosecutor's table, the Evidence Locker thrown open, exhibit after exhibit read into the record, and Alex in the dock with no lawyer, already sure of the verdict. Nobody was ever acquitted in there. The best you could hope for was a recess.

"See? There it is. You can put on the suit. You can get the degrees. You can learn to talk like the people in the meeting. But you've always been a kid playing dress-up, and he just saw the crayon on your collar. It was only a matter of time."

The first exhibit came the way it always did, not as a thought but as a smell. Jean Naté. Their mother's perfume. They were nine, standing in the kitchen with a drawing they had worked on all afternoon, a loud, crowded explosion of color they had been so proud of they ran the whole way home. Eleanor held it out at arm's length. She didn't yell. Yelling would have been easier. She let out a soft, tired sigh, the sound of a small hope leaving a room.

"Oh, honey," she said. "It's... a lot. You have so much energy. Maybe try to keep it inside the lines next time."

Inside the lines. She hadn't meant it as a wound. That was the hardest part. But a child doesn't hear the tone; a child hears the rule underneath it. And the rule Alex heard that day was simple: The real you is too much. To be loved, be less.

"Inside the lines, Alex," the Auditor said now, borrowing her softness. "That's all anyone has ever asked of you. Meet the deadline. Follow the rules. Be normal. And you can't. This isn't a mistake. It's a pattern. It's who you are."

Then another memory, colder. The principal's office at fourteen, floor wax and burnt coffee. They had been sent out of class for laughing too loud. The principal didn't look at them. He looked at a folder on his desk, their folder, as if it already held the verdict.

"I just got off the phone with your mother," he said. "She's at her wit's end. You're a smart kid, Alex. But you're becoming a problem. You need to decide what kind of person you want to be."

Same lesson, different room: you are a problem for other people to manage.

Alex sank further. The office went flat and gray. From somewhere above and behind their own head, they watched their hand pick up the phone. They watched their thumb scroll. They were looking for a hand to grab, any hand at all. They didn't call a therapist; they didn't have one. They didn't call the friend who would have picked up. Their thumb stopped on Tony.

Tony was not their boyfriend. He had never been their boyfriend. He had a boyfriend, who paid for the condo and everything in it, and Alex had a standing place at the back of Tony's life. He was the perfectly wrong person to call, and some part of Alex had always known it.

Tony gave attention in floods, the late-night calls, the three-hour nights in Alex's bed, the feeling of being the only person in the room, and then vanished for days without a word. That rhythm of flood and drought is the same unpredictable reward that keeps people feeding a slot machine. For someone who learned as a child that love arrived without warning and could be withdrawn the same way, a man like Tony didn't feel dangerous. He felt like home. It was the steady, available people who felt like a trick. Clinicians call this repetition compulsion: the pull to recreate an old wound in the hope that, this time, it will end differently.

It rang twice. "Hey, you've reached Tony. Leave a message." His voice was warm and careless and nowhere. The beep echoed in the quiet of their dissociation. Of course he wasn't there.

"Of course he didn't answer," the Auditor said. "People pick up for the ones who add something to their lives. You're a withdrawal, Alex. You're a problem to be solved, not a person to be loved."

Alex set the phone face down and sat very still. Not crying. Not anything. The email glowed on the screen like the light of a star that had already burned out. The hum was gone, and the pain was gone, and so, almost, was the sense of having a body at all.

This was the bottom they knew best. Not a place of screaming but of disappearing, where a nervous system that learned early how to survive unbearable feelings simply stops feeling. It had kept them alive once. It was the quietest, most efficient prison they had ever lived in.
