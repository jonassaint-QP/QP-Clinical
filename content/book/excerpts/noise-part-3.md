The way back into their body was not gentle. It started with their left foot, which had fallen asleep wedged against the leg of the chair. Pins and needles, a thousand tiny stitches pulling them back into their skin. Then the whine of the laptop fan, suddenly loud. Then color, bleeding back too bright under the fluorescent lights.

The pain came back with it. Not the sharp spike of the email, but a bruised, bone-deep ache, like the morning after a fever breaks: weak, clear, and aware of the damage.

The Auditor had gone quiet. What it left behind was worse. Just the facts. The deadline had been missed. The email had been sent. David had read it by now. No more voices, no more predictions. Only what was true. This was the most dangerous part of the cycle. The crisis was over. The evidence remained.

Their mind went looking for a way out. First, blame. Alex built the case like a prosecutor. He never puts deadlines in the shared calendar. He communicates in these clipped, passive-aggressive emails. How is anyone supposed to hit a target that keeps moving? It felt good for about a minute. Anger is easier to hold than shame; it gives you somewhere to stand.

Then they scrolled back through their inbox, and there it was, from two weeks earlier: Hi Alex, reminder that the Q3 report is due Thursday at 10. Let me know if you need anything. D. Clear. Polite. In writing. The case collapsed.

Next, shrinking it. It's one deadline. Everyone misses one. Sarah in marketing blew a launch date last month and nobody said a word. The Auditor answered in its most reasonable voice. "Sarah missed one. This is your third this year. That isn't a mistake, Alex. That's a pattern."

With the stories gone, only the feeling was left. And the feeling, like a song you haven't heard in years, took them straight back to the last time it had played.

Twenty-nine. A different office, an open-plan startup with a kombucha tap. A project they had championed had failed in front of the whole team because of a detail they'd missed, and their boss's face had gone still and cold. They remembered walking out that afternoon, not home but into the nearest dark bar. Two shots of cheap whiskey, back to back, not for a buzz but for a blunt object. The Auditor didn't go quiet. It only slurred. So they went home and opened their laptop and clicked through porn, tab after tab, not for pleasure but for noise, anything loud enough to drown out the louder thing inside. When that didn't work either, they opened the apps, and a stranger came over, and then a stranger left. They woke in gray light to an apartment that smelled of stale alcohol and sweat, the ache in their chest deeper than the one they had been trying to drown.

The memory let them go. Alex was back in the chair, the taste of phantom whiskey in their throat. It wasn't only a memory. It was a mirror. This was not a new crisis. It was a rerun. And reruns came on a schedule.

They had never said it out loud, but they could have drawn the graph. Every eighteen months or so, the whole system gave out. A new job, a new city, a new man, a burst of brilliance, eighteen months of white-knuckle competence, and then the fuse blew. Not a choice. Never a choice. Just the wiring, overloaded one time too many. They were nineteen months into this job.

They looked around the office as if seeing it for the first time. The framed degrees. The tidy desk. The shelf of books. It all looked like a set built for someone more competent to stand in.

The degrees first: a Bachelor's in Recreation Therapy, a Master's in Athletic Training, a second Master's in Social Work. They had been proud of them once. Now they saw what each had cost. The sleepless nights. The all-nighters on coffee and fear. The friendships lost, not in one big fight but one unanswered call at a time. The weddings they never made it to, a best friend's among them. The birthdays, the graduations, the nights that had mattered to someone else, all of it lost in a sea of sex, drugs, and alcohol. The constant dread of being found out. Not achievements. Receipts. Payments on an identity so impressive that no one would ever see the scattered, frightened kid underneath.

The Auditor knew exactly which receipts to pull. "Organic Chemistry," it said. "Remember? Every night at the kitchen table with four colors of highlighter. Notes so neat you could frame them. You never learned a thing. You just made the pages look like someone who studied." Alex remembered. An F, in a class they had organized beautifully.

"And Anatomy. Grad school, where a C is a fail. An eight a.m. class, forty-five minutes from home, and you walked in late so often the professor stopped looking up. The final project, started two days before it was due. Due at eight. Handed in at noon. Ten points off, and it wasn't even good work." The Auditor let it sit. "Two schools. Same kid. Same ending."

Then the books. Bowlby. Van der Kolk. Brené Brown. Sue Johnson. Linehan. Every one of them highlighted and dog-eared, notes crowding the margins. Alex could have explained attachment theory to anyone who asked. They could have drawn the nervous system's alarm states on a napkin. None of it had helped them in the last three hours. They were a lifeguard drowning in a room full of life jackets. They had the map. They were still lost.

The truth landed then, not as a thought but as a weight. Their working life was a performance, a fortress of competence built around a core of fear. Their relationships kept proving the same prophecy: chase the ones who disappear, push away the ones who stay. And every way they had tried to quiet the gap between the person they performed and the person they were, the whiskey, the porn, the strangers, had only made the gap wider. Hardest of all: the wound hadn't just hurt them. It had left them unable to take in the very love they were starving for.

They were so tired. Something in their body let go. A long, shuddering breath that seemed to start in the soles of their feet. Not peace. Surrender. They stopped bracing for the next blow. They stopped building the next excuse. They were just... there.

And in the space that surrender left, a small question pushed up through the rubble. What if there's another way?

The phone buzzed on the desk. For one stupid, shining second, they thought it was the answer. Tony: Sorry babe, back to back all day. Rough one? I'll swing by yours tonight. 7ish. Be ready for me.

The question sank back under the rubble without a sound.

It was astonishing how fast their body came back online for him. Ten minutes earlier Alex could barely feel their own feet. Now their heart was up and running, bright and quick, the way it always ran for Tony. Not hope, exactly. The slot machine lighting up. Their whole nervous system leaning toward the jackpot.

They shoved the laptop in their bag, told no one they were leaving, and were in the F-150 by twenty to four.

Getting ready for Tony had always been its own ritual, and Alex knew every step by heart. They scrubbed the bathroom tile on their knees until the grout came up white, because he had once made a face at the shower. They changed the sheets to the gray ones he liked. They wiped down every counter. They showered until the hot water ran out, shaved, trimmed, and stood in front of the mirror checking every angle like an inspection they expected to fail. They laid the harness out on the bed, then put it on, then took it off, then put it back on. They lit the candle he'd once said smelled expensive. They put on the playlist he liked, low. They ordered pad see ew from the Thai place on King, his favorite, so there would be food when he got there and he would never have to ask for anything. They set the phone face up on the counter with the volume all the way up.

Not one minute of it was for Alex. That was the point. If they were useful enough, ready enough, easy enough, he would stay. That was the deal. It had always been the deal.

They hadn't eaten anything real since morning. The pad see ew was for him. What they allowed themself was a fistful of sour gummies and a thirty-two-ounce Red Bull, standing at the counter: sugar and caffeine, enough to hot-wire the body without filling it.

Alex held master's degrees in Athletic Training and Social Work, and either one could have stopped this. The athletic trainer would have read the chart in a glance: hydration low, no real food since morning, adrenaline crash already underway, risk of syncope. Fluids, food, rest. The social worker would have been gentler and worse: client is abandoning their own basic needs to secure attention from an unavailable source. Establish safety. Feed the person.

But neither of them was on shift tonight. The only professional on the clock was the one who knew how to be useful, and to that one, hunger and thirst and rest weren't needs. They were obstacles. They got locked in the basement, gagged, so the performance could go on.

At 6:58 they were standing in the kitchen in the harness, breathing shallow, listening for the buzzer. Seven came. Seven-thirty. The pad see ew went cold in its bag on the counter.

At 8:15 they texted. Still coming? No pressure. Delivered. At 9:40: Here whenever you're ready. Delivered.

At eleven they stopped texting, because the Auditor pointed out, very reasonably, that three unanswered messages made you look desperate and four made you look insane.

They didn't take the harness off. Taking it off would mean admitting he wasn't coming. They sat on the edge of the bed with the phone in both hands and checked it, and checked it, and checked it. Every car door on the street was him. Every ding of the elevator in the hall. Every buzz they felt was only their own pulse.

Midnight. The playlist ran out, and they didn't restart it. The candle burned down to a puddle of wax.

At 1:07 the phone lit up. Tony: yo sorry fell asleep

Four words. No capital letters. No question mark, no next time, not even their name. Once, that would have been enough. When you're starving, a crumb is a meal. The old Alex would have typed no worries!! with two exclamation points, offered to drive over, and been grateful for the chance.

Alex looked at the screen and felt nothing reach for it. Not hope. Not even anger. Somewhere in the last six hours the hope had burned down with the candle, and what was left was the old, familiar, almost comforting proof. See? You're not worth showing up for. It wasn't I did something bad. It was I am unwanted. And under that, something quieter. They were too far gone to follow even this. There was nothing left in them that wanted the crumb.

They didn't answer. They unbuckled the harness and let it fall. The leather hit the hardwood with a soft, dead sound. They didn't cry. They were past that. They sat in the dark, on clean sheets nobody had slept in, in an apartment they had scrubbed for a man who had never once scrubbed anything for them.

They lay back across the bed, staring up at the still blades of the ceiling fan, and started bargaining. God, if you're there. Just make the brain stop. I'll be better. I'll be normal. Just take the noise away.

Jelly Roll's "Need a Favor" drifted up through the static, the one about a man who only calls on God when he's in trouble and knows exactly how that looks.

The Auditor laughed, a dry little sound in the empty room. "Transactional," it said. "Even your faith is a trade. You don't pray when you're designing sex swings. You only come crawling back when you're on the floor. You ain't got a prayer, kid."

And the question came back. Smaller now. But it came back. What if there's another way?
