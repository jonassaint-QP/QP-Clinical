Reaching for the laptop took everything they had. They opened a search window and sat looking at the blinking cursor. Even here, at the bottom, the part of them that had earned the degrees and read the books offered one useful thing: precision. If they could find someone who already spoke the language, attachment, ADHD, maybe they wouldn't have to spend the first three sessions explaining themself.

They typed: therapist attachment ADHD Toronto. The results were a wall of white faces, kind and serene and nothing like their own. The bios promised help "finding your best self" and "building coping skills," a language that had nothing to do with the last fifteen hours.

Where the faces weren't the problem, the logistics were. Not accepting new clients. Specializes in adolescents. Current waitlist: 18 months. Each one was a small, polite no. Together they said what the Auditor had been saying all along: No one is coming.

After an hour, Alex closed the laptop. The click was soft and final, like a gavel. Even when you finally ask for help, they thought, the world makes it impossible to get.

And then they did the thing their whole history had taught them never to do. They admitted to another person that they were not managing. They opened their texts, found Daddy Will, and typed three words. I'm not okay.

The dots appeared. Disappeared. Then the screen lit up with Will's face. He wasn't texting back. He was calling. After a night of silence and polite refusals, the ringing was almost a shock. Alex let it ring twice, shame coiling in their stomach, then answered.

"Hello?"

"Alex." Will's voice was thick with sleep, low and slow, so unlike the noise in their head that something in their chest dropped half an inch. "I got your text. Talk to me. Where you at?" Not a request for the whole story. Just: report your position.

"I messed up," Alex managed. "At work. It's happening again. The spiral. I can't..." Their voice broke.

"Okay," Will said. He had heard this before; he knew the sound of it. "I hear you. Now you listen to me. It's time. I'm sending you Dr. Jaxon's information. You remember me telling you about him?"

Alex remembered, barely. A conversation months ago, a name mentioned in passing that they had brushed off, sure they could handle things themself. Their silence answered for them.

"He's in the community, Alex," Will said. "He gets it. He gets our world. You're not going to have to explain the basics of your own life before you can get to the work."

It landed hard. Only then did Alex understand what the last hour of searching had really been. They hadn't just been looking for expertise. They had been looking for a room where they wouldn't have to translate themself, a need so constant and so rarely met that they had stopped noticing it. Some of today's exhaustion was the email. Some of it was thirty-eight years of straight rooms and white rooms, of code-switching, of explaining.

Will saw the next wall coming before Alex could build it. "And listen. He's not covered by OHIP. Before your brain starts in with that, stop."

"I can't afford it, Will."

"You're not hearing me." The love in his voice was as plain as the authority. "We have a line in our budget for you. For family. You're family. So there's no talking about this. It's done. All you need to do is click the link, fill out the form, and send it. I want this for you. I need this for you, because I love you. Your only job is to do the work. Let your chosen family handle the rest."

Chosen family. Alex knew exactly who he meant, because there were only five of them and Alex could have drawn the circle in the dark.

Will first. Daddy Will, who had earned the title the slow way, by showing up for years.

Clint, Will's partner, who shared his apartment and his balcony, who was Sir to them in the leather community, and who had sat with them through the worst health scare of their life and never once let them feel dirty for it.

Christian, the ex who had somehow become one of their best friends, the one who had sat beside them three years ago while a psychiatrist asked questions about attention and the answers finally started to make sense.

Chris, a hookup who had turned, over the years, into the closest thing they had to a brother.

And Sam, the only one of the five Alex had never slept with, who had known them longest of all. Since a rush mixer in Raleigh, when Sam was a nervous freshman who slipped and Alex caught them, twelve hours after the worst night of Alex's life. Their little brother in the fraternity, then one of their best friends, and by now the unofficial keeper of Alex's calendar. People who wanted Alex somewhere at a certain time had long ago stopped calling Alex. They called Sam.

And outside the five, because he had come before all of it, there was Shamus. Their best friend from kindergarten, three time zones away in Vancouver now, who they talked to every few months and who was, in every way that counted, their person.

Alex had no words. The family they grew up in had taught them that needing things made you a burden. In a few sentences, Will had taken away the money problem and the shame underneath it, and turned help into something you could receive instead of something you owed.

Clinicians call a moment like this a corrective emotional experience. In that office chair, it felt more like being hauled out of cold water by the collar.

They were crying now, but not from shame. From grief, and gratitude, and the strangeness of feeling both at once.

The phone buzzed against their ear: a link. "It's in your texts," Will said. "Click it. You can do that."

"Okay," Alex whispered.

"Good. Call me after. I love you." The line went quiet.

The Auditor made one last try. "He'll be disappointed in you too. You'll waste their money. You'll fail at this like you fail at everything." But its voice was thin now, a radio drifting off the station.

Alex clicked the link. A plain contact form. Name. Email. How can we help? Their fingers shook as they typed. I think I need help. Will referred me.

The panic was still there, the shallow breath, the pounding heart. But now it was being held inside something bigger. They weren't only doing this for themself. They were doing it for the people who had just said, out loud, that they were worth it.

They clicked Send. Thank you for your message. We'll be in touch within two business days.

There was no rush of relief. Just quiet where the Auditor had been. Not peaceful. Unfamiliar. The vertigo of standing at a door you have spent your whole life assuming was locked, and finding it open.

The silence in the room had changed. It was thinner now, and more fragile. It was the silence of a held breath. Of waiting. And in that waiting, for the first time in a long time, there was the possibility of something more.
