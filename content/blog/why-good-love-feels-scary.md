Things are finally going well. A sweet guy. A big win at work. And suddenly you want to burn it all down. Why?

Here's the short answer. **When your body is used to danger, safety can feel like a trap.** Good things push you past what feels normal. So a part of you tries to drag you back down. That part isn't evil. It's scared. Once you can hear it, you can stop letting it drive.

You're not broken. You're just not used to good. Let's talk about why, and how to stay when you want to run.

## A night out, and a good guy

Alex is in their late 30s and lives in Philly. Alex has ADHD and autism. Loud bars can be a lot. So Alex has learned to go out with a plan. Earplugs in their pocket. A friend to check in with. A time to leave, set before they walk in. (More on that in [Sensory Scaffolding](/blog/sensory-scaffolding-the-secret-architecture-of-a-sustainable-queer-life).)

On one of those nights in the Gayborhood, Alex met Dave. Dave was calm. He listened. He called when he said he would. Over a few weeks, it got real.

Then one night, Dave cooked dinner at Alex's place. He talked about the future. A real one, with both of them in it. And Alex's body went into full alarm. It felt like the walls were closing in.

Alex told Dave to leave. Then Alex reached for a hookup with someone they barely knew. It felt like nothing. Alex wasn't really there.

## The washed knife

The next morning, Alex walked into the kitchen. And they saw it. Before he left, Dave had washed the chef's knife. Dried it. Put it back.

Dave was hurt. He'd just been kicked out. And he still took three seconds to care for Alex's kitchen.

That tiny thing cracked something open. The mean voice in Alex's head, the one they call the Auditor, had a story. "Everyone leaves. You ruin everything. Nobody really cares." But the knife said something else. Somebody did care. Even then.

## The upper limit

A few weeks later, Alex got huge news at work. A new design line they'd dreamed up was a go. It was the biggest win of their career.

Within days, Alex was making choices with a guy named Kai that they knew would blow up. Alex could see it happening. They couldn't stop it.

Dr. Jaxon, Alex's therapist, had a name for this. A writer named [Gay Hendricks](https://hendricks.com/) calls it the **Upper Limit Problem**. We each have an inner thermostat for how much good we can feel. When life gets better than that setting, we find a way to cool things off. We pick a fight. We get sick. We self-sabotage. It's not that we don't want good things. It's that good things feel unsafe.

## Who the Auditor really is

Dr. Jaxon drew three circles. One said **Reasonable Mind**. That's the part of you that thinks in facts and lists. One said **Emotion Mind**. That's the part that feels everything, loud and fast. Where the two circles overlap, Dr. Jaxon wrote **Wise Mind**. That's the part of you that knows what's true, using both facts and feelings.

Then Dr. Jaxon asked Alex to close their eyes and find the Auditor.

Alex expected a judge. Instead, they saw a seven-year-old on a playground. The kid was trying to tackle grown-up Alex into the dirt. Not to hurt them. To hide them. Standing out had always meant getting hurt. So the kid's job was to keep Alex small.

The Auditor wasn't a villain. It was a scared little kid trying to keep Alex safe.

That's from Chapters 8 and 9 of [*Pathways to Presence*](/book).

## Why this isn't a character flaw

**Your body learned that good things come before bad ones.** If love was followed by pain when you were young, your body learned to brace when love shows up. That bracing kept you safe once. Now it gets in the way.

**Queer folks often learned to stay small.** Being seen was risky. So a lot of us grew a part that pulls us back when we shine. It's not weakness. It's old protection.

**Self-sabotage has a reason.** When you know why you do it, you can stop hating yourself for it. Then you can start doing something else.

**Joy can feel like a setup.** [Dr. Brené Brown](https://brenebrown.com/) calls this *foreboding joy*. Something good happens, and your brain jumps to picture it all going wrong. It feels like protection. But it steals the good moment. Her fix is gratitude. Notice the good thing, name it, and let yourself feel it, even for a few seconds.

## Skill 1: Find your Wise Mind

DBT is a set of skills for handling big feelings, created by [Dr. Marsha Linehan](https://www.linehaninstitute.org/). (New to it? Start with our [free DBT workbook](/resources/dbt-workbook).) **Wise Mind** is the calm, knowing part of you. Everyone has one. Even when you're in a panic, it's still in there.

**Pause and breathe.** Take three slow breaths. Let your breath out be longer than your breath in.

**Drop your attention low.** Picture your breath settling in your belly. That's where a lot of people feel Wise Mind.

**Ask the question.** Silently ask, "What does my Wise Mind say?" Then wait. Don't rush the answer.

**Notice what comes up.** It might be a word, a feeling, or a picture. Wise Mind is usually quiet and short. If the answer is loud and mean, that's probably the Auditor.

**Talk to the scared part.** If you find a scared part like Alex did, try being kind to it. "Thanks for trying to protect me. I've got this now."

**Try this script:** "Emotion Mind says run. Reasonable Mind says he's been nothing but kind. Wise Mind says stay, and tell him I'm scared."

## Skill 2: Make a squeeze signal

This one comes from EFT, or Emotionally Focused Therapy, created by [Dr. Sue Johnson](https://iceeft.com/). EFT looks at love as a bond. A big idea in EFT is that partners can help calm each other's bodies. That's called **co-regulation**.

Alex and Dave came up with a simple signal. They hold hands. **Two squeezes** means, "I'm activated, but I'm staying." **Three squeezes** means, "We need to step outside."

Here's how to make your own.

**Pick a signal.** Hand squeezes, a tap on the knee, or a code word. Keep it simple and quiet.

**Agree on what it means.** Say it out loud together. Make sure you both mean the same thing.

**Practice when you're calm.** Try it at home or on a walk. That way, your body knows it when stress hits.

**Honor it every time.** If your partner gives the "step outside" signal, go. No questions in the moment. You can talk later.

**Try this script:** "When I get flooded, I want to run. Can we have a signal that says 'I'm scared, but I'm not leaving'?"

## A song for the voices in your head

Chapter 9 is named for "Voices" by Chris Young. In it, he talks about the voices he still hears in his head. His dad, his mom, the folks who raised him. Their old advice guides him through life. Alex has voices too, and the loudest one was the Auditor. It's the pick because it shows that the voices in your head don't have to be enemies. Some of them come from love. Healing is learning which voice to listen to.
