You finally decided to talk to someone. Good. That part is genuinely hard, and you did it.

Then you open a directory, type "therapist Philadelphia," and get four hundred smiling headshots that all say "LGBTQ+ friendly." Some of them mean it. Some of them added the rainbow because a marketing course told them to. And you are supposed to figure out which is which before you spend $150 and an hour of your one wild and precious life.

This guide is for gay, bi, and queer men in Philadelphia and anywhere in Pennsylvania who want a therapist who won't need a glossary to understand their life.

## "Friendly" is not the same as "affirming"

An LGBTQ+ friendly therapist won't be shocked that you're gay. That's the floor, and frankly it's a low one.

An affirming therapist understands that the problem was never your identity. The problem is what you were taught about it: the shame you absorbed early, the way you learned to perform being fine, the scanning for safety in every new room. Affirming care treats those as real clinical material, not as background noise.

Here's a quick gut check. If you find yourself explaining what an open relationship is, what PrEP is, or why a family holiday wrecks you for a week, you are doing their homework on your dime.

## What to look for in a profile

- **Specifics, not slogans.** Look for named experience with gay and bi men: shame, coming out at any age, dating app burnout, sex and kink, open relationships, chemsex and substance use, HIV and PrEP, body image, family and faith.
- **A real license.** In Pennsylvania, look for an LCSW, LPC, LMFT, or licensed psychologist, and a license number you can look up with the state.
- **How they work.** Approaches like DBT, trauma-informed care, and somatic work show up a lot in queer-affirming practices because they deal with the body and with shame, not just thoughts.
- **Telehealth that's actually private.** Online therapy means anyone physically in Pennsylvania can see a Philly therapist, from Center City to the Lehigh Valley. Ask what platform they use and whether it's encrypted.

## Questions to ask on the first call

Most therapists offer a short consult call. Use it. You're interviewing them too.

1. **"How much of your caseload is gay, bi, or queer men?"** You want a real number, or at least a confident answer, not "oh, I see everyone."
2. **"How do you think about shame?"** Listen for whether they see it as something done to you, rather than something wrong with you.
3. **"Are you comfortable talking about sex, kink, open relationships, and drug use without judgment?"** Any flinch here is useful information.
4. **"What does a typical session look like with you?"** Some people want structure and skills. Some people want room to process. Both are valid. Know which one you're signing up for.
5. **"What does it cost, and do you take my insurance?"** Ask early. Money stress is a terrible way to start therapy.

## Trust the vibe (seriously)

Research on therapy keeps finding that the relationship between you and your therapist is one of the strongest predictors of whether therapy helps. If something feels off after a few sessions, it's okay to say so, and it's okay to leave. A good therapist will not take it personally. A great one might help you name exactly what didn't fit.
