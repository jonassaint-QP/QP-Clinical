It's the holidays. The family group chat is buzzing. Someone wants you home, and they want you to act like nothing happened. Your stomach is in knots.

Here's the short answer. **You're allowed to say no to family, and you don't owe a long reason.** A clear, kind no is a full answer. You can say it without a fight and without a speech. And saying no to a hurtful system is not the same as not loving anyone in it.

You're not broken. You're just tired of the smoke. Let's talk about why "no" feels so hard, and how to say it anyway.

## When the storm is over

Alex is in their late 30s and lives in Philly. For a long time, life was one crisis after another. Bad guys, big cravings, long nights. Then, slowly, things calmed down. Alex was sober. Alex was in therapy. Life got quiet.

And the quiet was awful. An ordinary Wednesday felt flat and gray. Nothing was on fire. So why did Alex feel so empty?

Dr. Jaxon, Alex's therapist, explained it. When you live in crisis for years, your body runs on stress juice. When the crisis ends, your body misses the rush. Calm can feel dull, even scary, at first. That doesn't mean something is wrong. It means you're healing.

Dr. Jaxon used a picture. Old habits are like a superhighway in your brain. Wide, fast, and easy. New habits start as a little footpath in the woods. Overgrown. Hard to find. Every time you walk the new path, it gets a little clearer. Every time you skip the highway, it gets a little more grass.

## A second name

Around this time, Alex got a big piece of news. At 39, they were tested for autism. The test came back yes. Alex has ADHD and autism, which some people call AuDHD.

It explained so much. The way loud rooms hurt. The way Alex always felt like they were acting a part. That's called **masking**. It means hiding your real self so you fit in. Alex had been masking their whole life. Now they had a name for why. If that sounds familiar, read [Sensory Scaffolding](/blog/sensory-scaffolding-the-secret-architecture-of-a-sustainable-queer-life).

## The email

Then the email came. It was from Julian, Alex's brother. Alex's mom was turning 75. There would be a party. Julian wrote that it was time to "let bygones be bygones." Alex should come home and keep the peace.

There was no sorry in it. No mention of the past. Just a quiet message: be there, and don't make waves.

Dr. Jaxon drew a triangle. It's called the **drama triangle**. It shows the roles people play in a hurting family.

**The persecutor** is the one who causes harm. In Alex's family, that was Dad.

**The victim** is the one who seems helpless. That was Mom, who always folded and never spoke up.

**The rescuer** steps in to smooth things over. That was Alex's brother Julian, who kept the peace by keeping everyone in line.

**The scapegoat** is the one who gets blamed. That was Alex. If Alex didn't show up, Alex would be the problem. If Alex did show up and said anything true, Alex would be the problem too.

That's called a **double bind**. You lose either way. The only way to win is to step out of the triangle.

## Writing the no

Alex didn't do this alone. Their friend Sam came over and sat with them while they wrote. Sam didn't write it for them. Sam just stayed in the room.

Alex wrote a short, calm reply. No long story. No list of every hurt. Just a clear no.

Then Sam said the thing that stuck. "You're not refusing to love them. You're refusing to walk back onto the lawn of a burning house where everyone pretends the smoke is normal."

That's from Chapters 6 and 7 of [*Pathways to Presence*](/book).

## Why this isn't a character flaw

**Families have roles, and leaving yours shakes the system.** When one person stops playing their part, everyone feels it. They may push hard to put you back. That push isn't proof you're wrong. It's proof you changed.

**Queer folks often get cast as the scapegoat.** If your family has trouble with who you are, you may be blamed for a lot that isn't yours. It's not fair. It's also really common.

**Boundaries are not punishment.** A boundary is about what you will do. It's not about controlling them. You can love your mom and still not go to the party.

**Clear is kind.** [Dr. Brené Brown](https://brenebrown.com/) likes to say that clear is kind, and unclear is unkind. A short, honest no is kinder than a vague maybe that keeps everyone hoping. You don't have to be harsh to be clear. That's exactly what the next skill helps you do.

## Skill 1: Say it with DEAR MAN

DBT is a set of skills for handling big feelings, created by [Dr. Marsha Linehan](https://www.linehaninstitute.org/). (New to it? Start with our [free DBT workbook](/resources/dbt-workbook).) **DEAR MAN** is a DBT tool for asking for what you need, or saying no, in a clear way.

**D is for Describe.** Stick to the facts. "I got your email about Mom's party."

**E is for Express.** Share how you feel, short and simple. "I feel sad, and I'm not ready."

**A is for Assert.** Say what you want, or don't want. "I'm not coming."

**R is for Reinforce.** Say why it can be good for both sides. "This way, the day can be about Mom, not about old fights."

**M is for Mindful.** Stay on track. If they bring up something else, go back to your point.

**A is for Appear confident.** Steady voice. No "sorry" every other line.

**N is for Negotiate.** Offer something, if you want to. "I'd be open to a phone call with Mom next week."

**Watch out for JADE.** JADE stands for justify, argue, defend, and explain. When you JADE, you hand them more to fight with. You don't need to win the debate. You just need to say your no.

**Try this script:** "Thanks for letting me know about Mom's party. I'm not going to come. I wish her a happy birthday. I'd be glad to call her next week."

## Skill 2: Get a body double

This one is an ADHD skill. A **body double** is someone who sits with you while you do a hard task. They don't do it for you. They're just there. For an ADHD brain, having another person in the room can make starting a lot easier.

Sam was Alex's body double. Here's how to set one up.

**Pick a calm person.** You want someone steady, not someone who will get fired up and take over.

**Say what you need.** "Can you just sit with me while I write this? You don't have to say anything."

**Set a timer.** Give yourself 25 minutes. Most hard emails take less time than the dread does.

**It can be online.** A video call works. So do online body doubling groups. Just having a face on the screen can help. Our free [ADHD Survival Guide](/resources/adhd-survival-guide) has more tricks like this.

**Try this script:** "I have a hard email to write. Can you hang out on video with me for 20 minutes while I do it?"

## A song for the outsiders

Chapter 7 is named for "The Outsiders" by Eric Church. It's a loud, proud song about not being part of the in crowd. The singer and his people stand outside the lines, and they like it there. That's where Alex landed. Their family cast them out as the problem. So Alex chose to stand with the outsiders, the friends who show up. It's the pick because it turns being left out into something strong. Being on the outside of a burning house is a pretty good place to be.
