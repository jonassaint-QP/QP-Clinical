You lead a whole team at work. You also can't make yourself open one boring email. So which one is the real you?

Here's the short answer. **Both are real, and you don't have to pick.** Your brain may tell you it's one or the other. You're either brilliant or a mess. That's a trap. The fix is one small word: **AND**. You can be gifted and still need help.

You're not broken. You're just tired of fighting yourself. Let's talk about why that little word matters so much.

## Alex walks into therapy

Alex is in their late 30s. They're a creative director in Center City. In [our last article](/blog/why-adhd-shame-makes-you-freeze), we saw Alex have a very bad Thursday. A late report, a wrong pronoun, a hot email, and a big freeze. That night, Alex asked for help.

A week later, Alex sat across from their new therapist, Dr. Jaxon. Alex talked fast. They said, "Look, I'm either amazing at my job or a total disaster. I can't figure out which."

Dr. Jaxon didn't argue. Instead, Dr. Jaxon asked Alex to go back further. Way back.

## The words that stuck

Alex told two stories.

**The dance.** When Alex was seven, they danced in the living room. They were spinning and happy. Their dad walked in and called them a "fairy." Their mom kept folding the laundry. She didn't look up. Alex stopped dancing that day. In a way, they never really started again.

**The coat.** Alex's mom once sewed them a patchwork coat. Alex loved it. The kids at school did not. They teased Alex until Alex cried. Then the principal made Alex say sorry. Not the bullies. Alex. Sorry for crying.

Those two days taught Alex two rules. Being yourself is dangerous. And your feelings are a problem. Alex has been following those rules for 30 years.

That's from Chapter 2 of [*Pathways to Presence*](/book). Now let's look at what those rules do to a grown-up brain.

## What's really going on

**One word can live in your body for years.** A word like "fairy" doesn't just hurt for a day. Your body files it away as danger. Later, a small comment from a boss can set off that same old alarm. You're not overreacting. You're reacting to the whole history.

**The mean voice grew up on those rules.** Alex calls the critic in their head the Auditor. The Auditor learned from Dad, the principal, and the kids at school. Its job is to catch every mistake before someone else does. It means well. It's also exhausting.

**Rejection can feel like a punch.** Lots of ADHD folks feel criticism way harder than other people do. Some call this RSD, or rejection sensitive dysphoria. That's a big name for a big pain. It isn't an official diagnosis. But many people with ADHD know it right away. We dig deeper in [ADHD, RSD, and the Auditor](/blog/adhd-rsd-the-biological-cost-of-the-auditor). A tiny "hmm" from someone can feel like the floor fell out.

**Either-or thinking keeps you stuck.** The Auditor loves "either-or." You're either great or worthless. Either you have it together or you're a fraud. When one mistake happens, the Auditor says, "See? Worthless." It wipes out all the good in one swipe.

## Why this isn't a character flaw

**Queer kids learn to hide early.** Alex wasn't punished for being bad. They were punished for being themselves. That's what happens to a lot of queer and trans kids. So we grow up thinking our true self is the problem. It isn't.

**ADHD makes it louder.** An ADHD brain already gets more "you forgot again" comments than most. Add the queer stuff, and the pile gets tall fast. Of course the Auditor got loud. It had a lot to work with.

**Both things are true about Alex.** Alex is very good at their job. Alex also misses deadlines. Neither fact cancels the other out. That's the whole lesson of this chapter.

**Shame tells you you're unworthy.** [Dr. Brené Brown](https://brenebrown.com/) has studied shame for years. She describes it as the painful feeling that we're flawed and don't deserve love or belonging. That's exactly what the dance and the coat taught Alex. Shame grows in silence. Saying it out loud, like Alex did in therapy, starts to shrink it.

## Skill 1: Use the word AND

DBT is a set of skills for handling big feelings, created by [Dr. Marsha Linehan](https://www.linehaninstitute.org/). (New to it? Start with our [free DBT workbook](/resources/dbt-workbook).) A big idea in DBT is that two things that seem opposite can both be true. DBT calls this a "dialectic." You can just call it **AND**.

Here's how Dr. Jaxon showed Alex. Dr. Jaxon went to a whiteboard and drew two columns.

On the left: "I'm a gifted creative director." On the right: "I struggle to start boring tasks." Then came a red marker. In the space between the columns, Dr. Jaxon wrote one big word: **AND**.

Alex stared at it for a long time. Nobody had ever let both be true before.

You can do this at home with a sticky note.

**Write your two truths.** Put the thing you're proud of on one side. Put the thing you're ashamed of on the other.

**Swap "but" for "and."** "I'm smart, but I'm a mess" makes the second part win. "I'm smart, and I struggle with deadlines" lets both stay.

**Say it out loud.** It will feel weird. Say it anyway. Your ears need to hear it in your own voice.

**Notice your body.** Does your chest loosen a little? That's the Auditor stepping back, even for a second.

**Try this script:** "I'm great at my job, and I need help getting started. Both are true."

## Skill 2: Check the rejection alarm

This one is an ADHD skill. When a comment stings way more than it should, your rejection alarm may be going off. The goal isn't to turn it off. The goal is to stop it from steering.

**Name it.** Say, "That's my rejection alarm." Naming a feeling helps your thinking brain come back online.

**Rate it.** From 1 to 10, how loud is it? If it's a 7 or more, don't make any big moves yet.

**Give it 20 minutes.** Don't reply. Don't quit. Don't text your ex. Go for a walk or drink some water. Big alarms get quieter with time.

**Ask one question.** "What else could this mean?" Maybe your boss was just busy. Maybe that short text was typed on a train. You don't have to believe the nicer story. You just have to know it's possible.

**Try this script:** "My alarm says they hate me. My alarm is loud, and it's not always right. I'll check back in 20 minutes."

## A song for the words that hurt

Chapter 2 is named for "Kill a Word" by Eric Church. In it, he wishes he could hunt down the cruelest words and get rid of them for good. Words that cut, that lie, that leave. For Alex, that word was "fairy," and it stuck around for 30 years. It's the pick because it gets how much power one word can carry. And it hints at the hope of this chapter. You can't erase the old word, but you can add a new one. Like AND.
